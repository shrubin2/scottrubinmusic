Clay Shaping as Performance

May 10, 2026

I’m writing this in anticipation of the premiere of a new work called Ancient Futures. This is an interdisciplinary collaboration between me and QuJie that centers clay-shaping as performance. The premiere is May 22 at Elastic Arts, and I wanted to take a moment to lay the background for this piece.

I’ve been wanting to explore the idea of “visual art as performance” for a while but struggled to find a good frame. My solo performance “Digital Hands” was originally a duo with saxophonist and visual artist Christopher Moore, where I tried to make a system where the visual artist would generate the sound through their gestures on the canvas. However, it gradually became evident that producing a drawing and compelling sound simultaneously was too much for one performer.

There are artists who do live-painting as performance, and there are dancers who use their bodies as paint brushes, but it seems that in these instances, one discipline takes dominance over the other.

I think the problem we kept running into was the permanence of the visual. We judge a canvas after paint has been applied. Does the canvas’s final character match the music that was created by the artist during the process?

What makes ceramics different from painting is that you can go through many create-destroy cycles. Rather than looking at a blank canvas, you’re looking at a chunk of wet clay. The canvas is the 3D space over the wheel, and you have the freedom to erase.

After music happens, its energy stays in your body, but we don’t see the air that was patterned. We can say the same about dance, but to a lesser extent. Dance performances are more likely to change the performance space visually than a music performance, but we don’t get to see traces of the dancers movements after they occur (unless the dancer used paint).

These ponderings lead me to seek out a dancer-ceramicist collaborator. Would it be possible to work with someone who could blend the physical languages of dance and clay-shaping. QuJie is a dancer and ceramicist in Chicago, and when I pitched the idea to them, they hopped onboard. More soon.
